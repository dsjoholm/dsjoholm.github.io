{% newthought 'Four days in Taipei' %}, two in Luodong, and two in Tamsui. I broke my time up like this to try to avoid spending the vast majority of the time in the capital city—sometimes amongst the most boring parts of a country—but this was a mistake. Because I found little for a tourist to do in Tamsui, I ended up heading into Taipei anyway during my time in Tamsui, so I wish that I had cut those two days in Tamsui and spent them in Taipei proper. The metro ride from Tamsui to Taipei was both longer—between 45 minutes and an hour depending on my destination in the city—and less comfortable than I imagined. It turns out that a lot of people board the MRT at Tamsui, the end of the line, and the next station, Hongshulin, which connects to the coastal LRT. As a result, the trains were crowded and hot. 

Overall, though, the MRT was one of the best public transit systems I have ever used, largely due to the very strong sense of public order and a mostly-unspoken-but-sometimes-explicit idea of “we’re all in this together.” Those two, of course, are closely related. 

## Public order on the metro

Queuing next to the platform for the train. Standing on the right, walking to the left on the  escalator. Leaving the priority seats and elevators for elderly, disabled, or pregnant women. Not making too much noise (or any noise, truly) on the train. Removing one’s backpack in crowded areas. Not littering. Not smoking. Paying the fare. One or two of these infractions of the social order don’t pose much of a problem, but a major problem in Western, and especially American, transit systems is that you can VERY OFTEN spot all of them at the same time. Over many trips and many experiences, this leads people to not-unreasonably believe that the whole social order is toothless and that common spaces like the metro are every man for themselves.

I’m sure that part of the reason for this comes down to cultural differences, but I’m not qualified to write about that, so I’ll leave it aside. To a tourist like me, the true and obvious difference is in enforcement. I would conservatively estimate that there are 20x as many visible staff members in the Taipei metro as compared to the Paris metro. They are everywhere—watching the fare gates, inside booths, on the platforms, and on the trains—and the people I saw barely seemed to notice their presence. In fact, “enforcement” is perhaps a bit of a misnomer for what they did: none of the police, security, or other miscellaneous staff ever enforced anything at all. Their presence was apparently enough to deter potential misbehavior, and crowds of riders streamed around staff with as much care as a river bending itself to avoid a rock.   

I also want to give a shoutout to the cleaning crew of the Taipei Metro, whose members seem to be able to spot even the smallest, least conspicuous piece of trash in order to maintain cleanliness. Their presence surely sets an example for riders. 

## Wandering and mascots

You can find a treasure behind every corner in Taiwan, and you barely need to spend any time or effort searching. Whimsy, a word I think is somewhat-overused but also captures a certain spirit and joie de vivre, is easily the best way to describe this, and the mascot-ification and cartoon-ification of absolutely every facet of Taiwanese life is the principal reason.

I think that there is a link between the omnipresent mascots and the ornamentation of buildings in Taiwan. A lack of ornamentation one of the reasons—exactly how important it really is can be debated—that most Americans and Westerners oppose new construction of just about everything. They find new buildings consistently uninspired, boring, and possibly hideously ugly. It’s hard to argue that they are wrong when comparing new and old construction in Paris.{% sidenote 'sn-id-23' 'Yes, there is a survivorship bias here. Fancier, better constructed buildings are probably more likely to stand the test of time. I do not think this accounts for all or most of the beauty gap between old and new construction. '%}

Taiwan’s buildings are highly ornamented, not only directly through their construction style but also because of posters, banners, and knickknacks that people have added to beautify them over time. Buildings are interesting and visually distinctive, which to me makes living in a dense city feel somehow more open and less crowded (this may be because always having something remarkable to look at takes my mind off of the density and crowds). I think this shows that ornamentation doesn’t have to be, like Paris’s beautiful façades, an expensive and time-consuming addition to buildings. It can be as simple as a fun poster or unique logo.
