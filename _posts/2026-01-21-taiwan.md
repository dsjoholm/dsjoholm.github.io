{% newthought 'Four days in Taipei' %}, two in Luodong, and two in Tamsui. I broke my time up like this to try to avoid spending the vast majority of the time in the capital city—sometimes amongst the most boring parts of a country—but this was a mistake. Because I found little for a tourist to do in Tamsui, I ended up heading into Taipei anyway during my time in Tamsui, so I wish that I had cut those two days in Tamsui and spent them in Taipei proper. The metro ride from Tamsui to Taipei was both longer—between 45 minutes and an hour depending on my destination in the city—and less comfortable than I imagined. It turns out that a lot of people board the MRT at Tamsui, the end of the line, and the next station, Hongshulin, which connects to the coastal LRT. As a result, the trains were crowded and hot. 

Overall, though, the MRT was one of the best public transit systems I have ever used, largely due to the very strong sense of public order and a mostly-unspoken-but-sometimes-explicit idea of “we’re all in this together.” Those two, of course, are closely related. 

## Public order on the metro

Queuing next to the platform for the train. Standing on the right, walking to the left on the  escalator. Leaving the priority seats and elevators for elderly, disabled, or pregnant women. Not making too much noise (or any noise, truly) on the train. Removing one’s backpack in crowded areas. Not littering. Not smoking. Paying the fare. One or two of these infractions of the social order don’t pose much of a problem, but a major problem in Western, and especially American, transit systems is that you can VERY OFTEN spot all of them at the same time. Over many trips and many experiences, this leads people to not-unreasonably believe that the whole social order is toothless and that common spaces like the metro are every man for themselves.

I’m sure that part of the reason for this comes down to cultural differences, but I’m not qualified to write about that, so I’ll leave it aside. To a tourist like me, the true and obvious difference is in enforcement. I would conservatively estimate that there are 20x as many visible staff members in the Taipei metro as compared to the Paris metro. They are everywhere—watching the fare gates, inside booths, on the platforms, and on the trains—and the people I saw barely seemed to notice their presence. In fact, “enforcement” is perhaps a bit of a misnomer for what they did: none of the police, security, or other miscellaneous staff ever enforced anything at all. Their presence was apparently enough to deter potential misbehavior, and crowds of riders streamed around staff with as much care as a river bending itself to avoid a rock.   

I also want to give a shoutout to the cleaning crew of the Taipei Metro, whose members seem to be able to spot even the smallest, least conspicuous piece of trash in order to maintain cleanliness. Their presence surely sets an example for riders. 

## Wandering and mascots

Whimsy, a word I think is somewhat-overused but also captures a certain spirit and joie de vivre, is easily the best way to describe this, and the mascot-ification and cartoon-ification of absolutely every facet of Taiwanese life is the principal reason.

there is a link between the omnipresent mascots and the ornamentation of
buildings in Taiwan. A [lack of ornamentation](https://worksinprogress.co/issue/the-beauty-of-concrete/) is one of the reasons—exactly how
important it really is can be debated—that most Americans and Westerners oppose
new construction of just about everything. They find new buildings consistently
uninspired, boring, and possibly hideously ugly. It’s hard to argue that they
are wrong when comparing new and old construction in Paris.{% sidenote 'sn-id-23' 'Yes, there is a survivorship bias here. Taiwan’s buildings are highly ornamented, not only directly through their construction style but also because of posters, banners, and knickknacks that people have added to beautify them over time. Buildings are interesting and visually distinctive, which to me makes living in a dense city feel somehow more open and less crowded (this may be because always having something remarkable to look at takes my mind off of the density and crowds). I think this shows that ornamentation doesn’t have to be, like Paris’s beautiful façades, an expensive and time-consuming addition to buildings. It can be as simple as a fun poster or unique logo. 

A great example of fun ornamentation on a building is this slide leading down from the Sanchong train station. Does it serve a particular practical end? No, but it is unique and memorable.

## Taipingshan National Forest

My sole purpose in schlepping to Luodong was to be able to easily visit Taipingshan National Forest, nestled high in the mountains of Northern Taiwan. The city of Luodong was unremarkable, and I have little to say about it.

{% marginfigure 'mf-id-2' 'assets/img/IMG_2211.jpeg' 'Except for this taco stand. Is this a joke? I couldn’t tell. ' %}

To reach Taipingshan, one can either drive or take the 1750A bus leaving from Luodong Bus Station (about a 5 minute walk west from the rail station). Having no car, I took the bus. It cost about $425NTD round-trip, which I consider a very good deal for a long ride on a comfortable coach bus. I bought the tickets (cash or credit card only for the 1750A, no Easy Card) from the counter at the bus station. The main downside of taking the bus is that it leaves you wth only four hours at the park, which was reduced to three and a half the day I went because of some kind of small landslide high up on a mountain road. I didn’t feel too pressed for time, though, even after spending an hour eating my lunch at the cafeteria. 

Taipingshan was almost empty when I visited, which combined with the fog and altitude of nearly 7,000 feet made for a quiet, somewhat brooding atmosphere. The main loop, located at the top of a section of nearly 500 stairs, is easy and has some interesting signage about native Taiwanese flora and fauna. Apparently, this is one of the last remaining native high-altitude forests on the island. After that, I walked about a mile to the Jiancing path, which follows a shuttered rail line once used for transporting timber. I enjoyed both trails, although they bore much more resemblance to guided walks than to hikes.

## Houtong Cat Village

Houtong is a small, mountainouse town on the rail line between Taipei and Luodong. It used to a be a coal-producing area, but when that industry dried up it seemingly decided to reinvent itself as a cat-themed tourist village. This was very successful! There are lots of friendly cats wandering around, sleeping, and sunning themselves, and nearly every business in the village has cat-related food and drinks. It is also centered around a beautiful gorge with a stunning bridge. This was a nice place to spend an hour reading in the company of an amical cat. 

{% maincolumn "assets/img/IMG_2202.jpeg" "The aforementioned cat" %}

The several museums about coal production in the area seemed interesting as well, but I didn’t visit any of them. 

## Food

Saying something meaningful about the food in Taiwan is difficult for me; almost everything contains meat, and I am vegetarian, so I had to carefully plan and search for specific restaurants in advance. Vege Creek near the Zhongshan metro station was terrific and cheap, and I ate there a total of four (!!) times during my seven days in Taiwan. You select the ingredients you want—I always picked at least one noodle, fake meat, leafy green, and mushroom—and the restaurant cooks them into a delicious soup. It’s highly warming and comforting food. 

{% maincolumn "assets/img/IMG_2111.jpeg" "My first Taiwanese meal" %}

I also went to several vegan buffets, which are simple because, well, you know that everything is vegan. These buffets seem to be mostly run by Buddhists, and nothing was ever labeled so I had no idea of what food I was selecting. I didn’t like them. The vegetables and tofu are often not very warm and a lot of the food is slimy in an unpleasant way. Cheap food can’t be beat, though, and the customers were very nice to me despite not speaking any English at all. In one buffet; a woman embarrassingly pointed out to me that I was using the wrong end of the chopsticks and gave me some tips; in another, a man showed me where to find the free soup. {% sidenote 'sn-id-24' '

## Attractions

My main attraction was wandering. It’s difficult to become bored when walking around, especially in Taipei. My favorite museum I visited was the 228 Museum, which gives an overview into and tells personal stories from the 228 Incident, a 1947 near-uprising and protest movement turned massacre, and the White Terror, a 38-year period of repression of democracy on the island. The museum’s displays were almost entirely in Mandarin, but Google Translate made it easy to decipher the main points. The best part was that the greeter, an elderly woman, welcomed me nicely and absolutely lost her mind in excitement when I thanked her with xièxiè. She started shoving different pamphlets and magazines into my hands and refused to take no for answer. 

I also went to the National Museum, which had some nice nature and history exhibits, along with this ridiculously racist caricature of indigenous Taiwanese. The coverage and insight into the native groups were the strong point of this museum. 

{% maincolumn "assets/img/IMG_2153.jpeg" "Poster" %}

The Chiang Kai-Shek memorial complex took my breath away when I turned the corner and saw it for the first time. It, along with the gate opposite it and the theaters on either side, are impressively large and imposing.  

## Travel

I flew with Air China in both directions, each with a stop of differing lengths in Beijing. The flights were ordinary and unremarkable. Both long-haul flights (from Paris to Beijing and Beijing to Paris) had seatback TVs with a mediocre-to-bad selection of Western movies, some of which had Mandarin subtitles that could not be disabled. One, Now You See Me 2, was dubbed into Mandarin with no English subtitles or ability to switch to an English audio track! 

The main “benefit” of flying with Air China or another Chinese airline when heading to East Asia is the ability to cut several hours off of the flight time by passing through Russian airspace. This is convenient, as Western planes are taking noticeably more circuitous routes to the north. I suspect this direct routing also leads to some amount of monetary savings when booking with Air China or similar.

## Miscellaneous 
- There are a lot of crossing guards, even at intersections and driveways that really don’t seem to me to be particularly dangerous. I wonder if this is some kind of jobs program.
- The absence of children is particularly visible: Taiwan’s fertility rate is at massive-crisis level. I could see how the strong social norms of absolute quiet and order in public spaces could discourage people having children for fear of sticking out and being rude.
- Night markets are a great vibe and have very good food.
- The density of commercial spaces, especially restaurants, is incredibly high. Most blocks in urban areas have multiple restaurants, which are often very small and serve just a few dishes. In general, eating at restaurants seems like a very common, almost daily, occurrence for residents.
- The pond near the Chiang Kai Shek Memorial has a vending machine for fish food. Feeding them was a calm way to pass 10 minutes or so.
