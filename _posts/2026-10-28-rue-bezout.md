## The bust

A wanderer exploring the streets of Paris will find many oddities, curiosities, and disparate building styles amongst the densely packed buildings. If they are looking very closely—and visit exactly the right addresses—the wanderer might also discover busts of a seemingly historical figure, possibly from the IIIème republic period (1870-1940). The man, shown from the chest up, has a traditional French suit and tie, a round face, and an angular moustache. In many ways, he appears very similar to other semi-anonymous statues one might vaguely notice but never really investigate around Paris. Our wanderer might assume that, if they looked closely, they would find a plaque explaining that he was head of a bank, or a chief engineer of some project, or a local politician with a few modest achievements. Except this man does not exist. He never existed; yet, his visage still adorns a few lucky buildings throughout Île-de-France.

![Our mysterious man, perched on high. Photo by Phoebe Skok](bust1.jpg)

## 23 rue Bezout

One of the buildings over which this bust surveils is 23 rue Bezout, an apartment building in the 14ème arrondissement near the southern edge of Paris. It sits on the corner of rue Bezout and rue Hallé, and hosts the addresses 23 rue Bezout and 78-80 rue Hallé. I’ll refer to it as the former for simplicity, but the building is one and the same. I became interested in the building because it is very clear to me that it is out of place and essentially unique in Paris. While most Parisian buildings resemble each other quite strongly—they are tan with zinc roofs, 5 or 6 stories tall, have detailed ornamentation on balconies and overhangs, and have relatively little coloration—23 rue Bezout stood out to me. [^color] Its exterior is tile, mostly a bright white with flashes of yellow and deep blue. The window frames are a vibrant red. There are no real balconies, but certain windows and corners protrude from the building at unusual right angles. Several of the windows are round and playful, and most of the windows (round or not) are spaced irregularly. In front, there is a small garden in a space that clearly used to be a fountain. The main doorway is flanked by two columns, as is the garage entrance. On one of the columns outside of the garage rests the bust, perched somewhat awkwardly above shoulder height. 

![23 rue Bezout. Photo by Phoebe Skok](bez3.jpg)

![Another angle. Photo by Phoebe Skok](bez1.jpg)

I walked past this building for months, always vaguely wondering to myself why it seemed so different from every other Parisian building I knew. It turns out that the answer to this question involves our recurring bust. But to properly answer it, we’ll first need some context. [^5] 

## Paris, circa 1900

Paris in 1900 was a [dirty place](https://passerelles.essentiels.bnf.fr/fr/metier/35fb54a0-af3b-43c6-8682-ee21e779620d-carreleur/article/528f0fab-166a-4bbe-b56c-1abe5c7ca5c8-carrelage-en-exterieur-immeuble-henri-sauvage-6-bis-rue-vavin). Residents were, for good reason, terrified of disease and epidemics. The city was also welcoming its first metro lines, built for the 1900 Paris Exposition (similar to a World’s Fair). Paris metro stations opened with white tiled surfaces—along with some other tones—because white was viewed as a clean, hygienic contrast to the often blackened and soot-filled environment around the city. White tiles were also able to best make use of the brand-new [electric lightbulbs](https://www.ratp.fr/decouvrir/patrimoine/les-frises-du-metro) installed in stations. In this era, lightbulbs were not particularly strong, and clean white tiles were needed to reflect the light. Architect Hector Guimard led the effort to install these tiles, produced by the company Boulenger.  

The white tiles gave the impression of cleanliness and largely pleased Parisians, including another young architect named Henri Sauvage. Sauvage was made famous by his 1902 Villa Majorelle, a country house seen as a good example of the Art Nouveau style. Between 1912 and 1914, he and Charles Sarazin designed a radically new and hygienic 6 story apartment building at 26 rue Vavin in the 6ème arrondissement. This type of construction was intended to serve as a radical solution to unsanitary conditions in the city; it aimed to bring more light and fresh air into apartments while allowing plants to grow in and around windows. Its exterior is composed of the [same white tile](https://www.paris.fr/pages/5-edifices-remarquables-d-henri-sauvage-architecte-de-la-samaritaine-17993) that can be found in the Paris metro along with some blue tiles to serve as [accent colors](https://vertigesdelart.net/2020/05/15/henri-sauvage-et-limmeuble-a-gradins-de-la-rue-vavin-paris-6/). Sauvage then went on to separately design a pool room—then private, now open to the public—in which not only the pool but the floor, walls, and ceiling were covered in striking white, blue, and green tiles [^pool]. These clean, vibrant colors made Paris appear [cleaner and more beautiful](https://www.telerama.fr/sortir/henri-sauvage-l-architecte-qui-a-rendu-paris-plus-propre-et-plus-beau-7015579.php) to the general public. Maybe it’s just me, but I can see traces of the Art Déco movement that would arise in the 1920s in this style. In addition, the blue and white tile is very similar to that seen at 23 rue Bezout…

## ED Architectes 

But before we come back to the unique building in the 14ème, we’ll take a pit stop at the 1960s École des Beaux Arts de Paris, where six young men— Daniel-Lucien Bertrand, Patrick Demanche, Philippe Dornier, Jean-Jacques Pauzat, Alain Peskine and Jean-Pierre Roullé—met for the first time. The six young graduates would go on to found the architecture firm ED Architectes in 1970. The group described themselves as closely-knit and highly collaborative; Peskine would [write that](https://web.archive.org/web/20250317121237/https:/www.ceacap.org/billet-n-179-ed-kit-et-le-concours-des-mille-clubs-de-jeunes-1-2/) with respect to his firm, “the imagination of a single person will never replace the clash of the imaginations of many.” 

Importantly, the group came of age during the May 1968 protest movement that brought French society to a halt and had profound impacts on those who were students at the time. The group’s penchant for teamwork was at least somewhat born out of post-1968 ideas of communal living and working. They used the famous protest slogan “Il est interdit d’interdire,” or “It is forbidden to forbid,” in their architecture practice, and attempted but failed to set up international branches. Still, they took inspiration from overseas ideas and trends; notably, they were inspired by the American group [SITE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SITE), which advocated for combining nature with architecture and introducing intentional cracks or crumbles into walls.[^site] In the 1970s, they spent several years working out of an “Ed-Kit,” a modular and flexible building meant to appeal to French governments needing housing and social facilities to keep up with demand from the baby boom and Trente Glorieuses, the long economic boomtime following World War II. Mostly, the firm worked on housing and offices in the Paris area. It closed its door permanently in 2020; by that point, [none of the original founders were at the company](https://edarchitectes.net/).

ED Architectes’ style was, to my untrained eye, highly playful to the point of being whimsical. Plants stick out against white and blue tiles while red adornments attract attention. They had a philosophy: [L’architecture est faite pour qu’on la regarde](https://www.edarchitectes.net/site/livre/livre_habitat_edarchitectes_2011.pdf). Architecture is made so we can look at it.   

## Back to 23 rue Bezout

Finally we arrive back to 23 rue Bezout. It was [constructed in 1987](https://www.bercail.com/paris/75014/rue-bezout/23) after the demolition of one or two previously existing buildings on the same site. This part of rue Bézout dates to the Haussmannian period, when it was cut through existing buildings. The neighborhood still boasts many examples of 1-2 story faubourg-style buildings that were common in Paris in the early-mid 1800s. Many of them predate the 1860 annexation of Montrouge by Paris. [^1] I was unable to confirm this, but it is likely that the current building replaced a structure similar to those. 

![The type of buildings that may have been demolished to make way for 23 rue Bezout](the-type-of-buildings-that-may.jpg)

In my opinion, it is possible to draw a straight line from the Paris metro’s white tiles at the turn of the century to Henri Sauvage’s white tiled apartments to the Mai 1968 protests to the building at 23 rue Bezout. The architects at ED Architectes certainly were aware of Sauvage’s work, and the atmosphere during which they studied clearly influenced their sense of playfulness, joy, and fun in architecture. This is why we see small, unusual architectural quirks such as windows jutting out from the building at a 45 degree angle. This is why we see the roof change direction and give way to a small tile-covered terrace. This is why we see, in a break from tradition, the tiles covering the building’s entire exterior; it is also why we see these tiles make random slanting patterns and geometric shapes. This is why the windowsills are painted bright red. The architecture style here is—as we’ll see later—best described as postmodern, and the architects embrace ornamentation for its own sake. The apartments at 23 rue Bezout are fun, and they’re proud of it. [^4]

Their most striking architectural work can be found at 15 rue de Vanves, in Issy-les-Moulineaux. This small housing development, which was deservedly honored with a prize in 1986, rises like a spaceship, completely at odds with yet strangely in tune with its surroundings. It, like 23 rue Bezout, has a white tile exterior with blue highlights, but in this project the blue is slightly closer to a green like the color of the ocean on a stormy day. It is a beautiful sight, made even better by the abundant plant life enthusiastically spilling over the walls and out of balconies. The apartments’ curved and angular lines contrast nicely, and the arched doorway with a bright red metal door seems to welcome the visitor into an aquatic kingdom. 

At 5 rue de l’Eure, we see a totally white tile facade with no other ornamentation. This one isn’t my favorite, but it does feature another example of a bust. We’ll get there!

20 rue de l’Avre has, somewhat uniquely for ED Architectes, very little tile. Its only presence is a 6 story tall column lined with navy blue tiles that cuts through residents’ balconies. It’s striking, and it stands close to another bust. This one is mounted in a marble frame and sits above a highly detailed and impressive carved horse’s head. 

10 rue de Meudon, in Clamart, is uniquely constructed around a 19th century tower. The architects decided to conserve the tower and other historic buildings while adding 12 white-tiled apartment buildings, pedestrian walkways, and green spaces. Another bust stands guard in front of the complex. 

17 rue du Renard is notable mostly for the way in which it incorporates a large mosaic artwork on its outward-facing wall. The mosaic is well integrated into the building and provides a flair to the view. 

The final ED Architectes building in this style that I could identify is at 11bis rue Pierre Nicole. It incorporates a white tile exterior with an interestingly jagged façade and bright-red windowsills. By far its most notable feature, though, are the ruins on the ground floor which give the impression that the building was placed directly on top of, or perhaps is growing out of, a Roman hearth. These ruins are not authentic. They were constructed along with the rest of the building in 1980. Behind the faux-Roman ruins is a panorama painting that shows two modern tourists taking a photo in front of a Parisian-style mansion. According to the artist and observers, the scene represents a future Paris that has suffered a tragic, destructive event similar to Pompeii. In the distance, we see angels performing distinctly modern activities: watching TV, eating McDonald’s, playing tennis, grilling a hamburger. These are to show that our current world, inhabited by "homo consumator," will one day be no longer, just as Pompeii crumbled under a volcano’s power. 

The artist and painter who built the ruins and mural, Ngoc Duong Varoqui, says that her piece is a fiction that tells about our world and a critique that announces its imminent destruction.

![The "ruins" at 11bis rue Pierre Nicole](the-ruins-at-11bis-rue-pierre.jpg)

There is a contrast here between the modernity (tiles, a modern-style building) and Parisian history (ruins). Just down the street, at 14bis rue Pierre Nicole, if one takes a dilapidated set of stairs far deeper than the lowest level of the underground garage, one finds the Notre-Dame-des-Champs crypt. The space is eerie and dark. It is here that Saint Denis is said to have baptized the very first Parisians into Christianity, starting, in a way, modern French culture.[^2] Returning to 11bis of the same road, we see the envisaged end of what that culture has become.

![The very underground crypt. Today it is about 50 feet subsurface, but it may have been just under the surface during the time of Saint Denis.](the-very-underground-crypt-today-it.jpg)

One of 11bis's architects, Patrick Demanche, has lived in the building he designed since its completion. He lives in a triplex on the 4th-6th floors, with a panoramic view of Paris’ left bank. He seems to view the architectural choices as a kind of joke: a lie that the building is built on real ruins. It’s tongue-in-cheek (au second degré, as the French would say), and it is at once funny, pleasing, and a bit frustrating. 

This playfulness and humor is, in my mind, the defining feature of ED Architectes’ work of this type in Paris. While researching this article, I came across a [2023 book](https://www.lesparisdld.com/2023/09/paris-postmoderne-architectures-1973.html) by Jean-Louis Violeau called Paris Postmoderne : Architectures 1973-1993. Gracing its cover is 11bis rue Pierre Nicole. [^book] In the book, Violeau cites at least three buildings I have noted as good examples of Parisian postmodern architecture during this time period. He describes this style as mixing influences of different periods, while always making originality the top priority. This can be seen in its playfulness and its tendency towards interesting angles and bright colors; it is a rejection of a cold and boring modernism. Architects building in this style make winks at the past and use fantastical elements. To the famously modern architect Mies van der Rohe’s assertion that “less is more,” a Parisian postmodern designer would reply, “less is a bore!”

## Elmer Dwight Édouard

This brings us all the way back to the busts that we discussed at the beginning of this article. They stand on poles and pillars outside of several ED Architectes-designed buildings in Paris, but the man depicted in them does not exist. Who is he, then?

![The bust at 10 rue de Meudon](the-bust-at-10-rue-de.jpg)

![The bust at 20 rue de l'Avre, sorry for low quality image](bust-avre.png)

He is Elmer Dwight Édouard. The character has no historical basis but was created—backstory, appearance, and all—from scratch by the architects at ED Architectes. An entire fictional story was created around him, and the bust was displayed on the façades of many buildings designed by the firm. The goal was to give the small company an identity; apparently, this idea worked quite well and proved able to attract clients for developments around Paris. He is somewhere between a practical joke, an advertisement, and a wink and nod at France’s penchant for displaying semi-obscure historical statues at every opportunity. How perfectly fitting for a group of architects who liked nothing more than having some fun. [^3]
