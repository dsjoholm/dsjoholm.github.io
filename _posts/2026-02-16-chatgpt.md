{% epigraph 'The market can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent.' 'John Maynard Keynes' %}

Universities are in a strange place. Use of LLMs like ChatGPT, which is still the only AI tool normal people and students have ever heard of, 
has become absolutely rampant on campuses. In 2025, the number of students who self-reported that they used AI tools to help with their 
[schoolwork was 92%](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2025/). That's very, very high, and it's worth remembering
that this technology was niche and unknown only a bit over three years ago. The last tech tool with such stunningly fast market penetration
in an educational setting was probably...Facebook, which according to [this charmingly innocent article](https://www.hepi.ac.uk/reports/student-generative-ai-survey-2025/)
reached 85% usage in US colleges within a year and a half of launch.{% sidenote 'sn-id-cheating' 'I would guess Facebook and ChatGPT are probably used for different types of cheating, though.'%} 

ChatGPT and Anthropic, maker of Claude, acknowledge that cheating is happening but mostly claim that their tools are primarily used by students to ["develop skills of rigorous thinking and clear writing"](https://chatgpt.com/use-cases/student-writing-guide/) and ["create and improve educational content across disciplines"](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropic-education-report-how-university-students-use-claude).
This is obviously BS. It suffices to walk around any university library or study hall to see students directly copying-and-pasting paragraphs or answers
from ChatGPT before immediately moving on to the next question. ChatGPT use plummets over summer break, then skyrockets when school is back 
in session.{% marginfigure 'mf-id-gpt' 'assets/img/2026-02-01/CG_Usage.jpg' 'Trends in ChatGPT usage' %} This occurs to such an extent that you one tell exactly the dates on which those occur by merely glancing at a usage chart. I don't even mean to condemn anyone—it's
true that AI use helps people finish assignments faster and get to other things they might rather be doing. But its widespread use certainly
gives university a less educational vibe. Everyone knows that everyone else is cheating, but few people talk about it and nobody has any way to truly be certain in any individual case.

So why haven't universities done anything? By "anything," I don't only mean "crack down really hard on AI use." That is one response—blue books and handwritten examsstill work perfectly well—but another option is to embrace AI, or even mandate its usage in classes and weave it into the larger curriculum and goals of the university. Ohio State University and the University of Florida have taken this path: OSU says that by 2029, every student will "be fluent in applying artificial intelligence responsibly and effectively."{% sidenote 'sn-id-elite' 'As an aside, I think it's worth noting that the universities adopting publicly AI are universally not the high-prestige, high-status Ivy Leagueschools and their ilk. Amongst the kind of elite students, staff, and faculty at these universities, AI use is coded as low status and possiblyeven right wing. It's seen negatively due to [mostly-fake concerns about water use and environmental harm](https://andymasley.substack.com/p/the-ai-water-issue-is-fake) and [somewhat-real concerns about stealing copyrighted work from others](https://ipwatchdog.com/2025/06/24/judge-calls-anthropics-training-llms-authors-works-quintessentially-transformative-gives-no-pass-piracy/).It's my sense that it would be somewhat uncomfortable, possibly gauche, to be strongly pro-AI in this milieu.'%}

But mostly universities are just muddling through. They put out statements and release policies about how they're definitely, really, absolutely taking
the problem of AI cheating seriously, then punish several handfuls of students at most for it. Professors are told to make it clear that they won't tolerate
AI work, but everyone knows the whole exercise is a charade—AI "detectors" [don't work at all](https://mitsloanedtech.mit.edu/ai/teach/ai-detectors-dont-work/) and 
attempts to convince students to not use AI come off as hopelessly naive. In the last link, MIT recommends reminding learners that "OpenAI’s terms of use state that users may not “Represent that Output was human-generated when it was not.” Really? Who cares?

Universities seem mostly torn—they know AI is useful, fast, and in practice undetectable, but they can't say that out loud. What reason would 
they have to exist? Like irrationality in the stock market, this charade will continue for a long time into the future, even as predictions 
that the whole house of cards will crumble come and go. Universities have built up strongly institutional perceptions of who they are and what purpose they serve, and those
perceptions will take years to shift. They are mostly staffed by people who think the same way as one another, and rocking the boat comes
at too high of a personal cost. Muddling through is easier for everyone. They won't make dramatic moves towards in-person exams or some sort of
harsh crackdown on at-home AI use; they also, with a few more exceptions, won't fully embrace AI. Like a huge ship trying to turn on a dime, they
are simply unable to do so. 

Because of leftover cultural prestige, universities will of course continue to be worthwhile and peopled by eager new students for the foreseeable future. 
That goodwill is [already slipping away](https://www.pew.org/en/trend/archive/fall-2024/americans-deepening-mistrust-of-institutions), though. And, a college education already derives [most of its value](https://scottaaronson.blog/?p=3678) from
it acting as a filtering mechanism—anyone who gets a degree has some level of knowledge and will put in at least a baseline amount of effort into completing tasks—but
what if this stops being the case? Employers could catch on that it has become basically effortless to "earn" a degree, and we might finally reach the other 
side of Keynes' famous quote about market irrationality.
